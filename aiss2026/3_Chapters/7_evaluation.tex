\section{Evaluation Framework}\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluating a multi-agent conversational system means checking several things at once. Does it call the right tools? Does it ground its answers in fetched data? Does it keep a supportive tone, and does the user reach their goal across multiple turns? No single method covers all of this. A deterministic metric cannot judge conversational quality, and a short usability test cannot characterise the agent's behaviour over many free-form conversations. The evaluation therefore consists of \textbf{two complementary studies} with the same participant group. The \textit{usage study} (Sections~\ref{sec:evaluation:framework}--\ref{sec:evaluation:metrics}) analyses the logged conversations real users had with the deployed system. It scores the LLM's behaviour with LLM judges and a deterministic grounding scorer. The \textit{usability study} (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:usability}) adds structured tasks and standardised usability measurement with the same users. Beneath both sit \textit{operational reliability metrics} (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:reliability}): tool-call success rates, upstream API failure rates, and latencies, all aggregated from the same execution traces the scorers read. This measures architectural reliability under real usage, not in a separate synthetic test level. One scope decision frames all of this. Assessing the sports-science \textit{quality} of a generated training plan is not a primary focus of this evaluation. Validating it would require sports-science expertise and longitudinal observation beyond the scope of this seminar project. The core question is instead whether the agentic system reliably selects and calls the correct MCP services and grounds its answers in the retrieved data.

\subsection{Usage Study: Evaluation Pipeline}\label{sec:evaluation:framework}

The usage study scores the conversations real people had with the deployed Training Copilot. An earlier iteration simulated synthetic user personas, with an LLM role-playing the user. We replaced it with real-user logs. A persona simulator ultimately measures the system against an LLM's \textit{imitation} of a user. Logged conversations measure it against genuine user behaviour, vocabulary, and impatience. The trade-off is that we lose scripted control over domain coverage, which we account for in the reporting (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:metrics}). We build on MLflow's multi-turn evaluation tooling~\citep{mlflow_multiturn_2025}. Every chat turn is traced live into the user's own MLflow experiment and tagged with a session identifier. This groups turns into complete conversations that session-level scorers can assess as a whole.

The LLM-judge scorers rest on a finding by \cite{zheng_judging_2023}: a strong model grading open-ended dialogue agrees with human preferences about as often as humans agree with each other. This makes LLM-as-a-judge a scalable proxy for human evaluation. Their catalogue of judge biases (position, verbosity, self-enhancement) also motivates our model-separation safeguard (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:models}). The design further draws on \cite{shankar_validates_2024}, who show that LLM evaluators must be aligned with human preferences to be reliable. Our deterministic grounding scorer follows the $\tau$-bench approach of \cite{yao_taubench_2024}, which judges tool-agent interaction by comparing system state against annotated goals rather than chat text alone. The pipeline has three components. A \textbf{per-user trace store} holds each user's conversations, recorded live during normal use. A set of \textbf{scorers} combines an LLM judge for the conversational dimensions with a deterministic code checker for grounding. An \textbf{experiment tracker} (MLflow) records every conversation, trace, verdict, and metric. The pipeline is fully automated. A single command (\texttt{python -m evaluation.run\_users}) reads every user's logged conversations, scores them, and generates an HTML report with per-user scorecards.

\subsection{Real-User Conversation Corpus}\label{sec:evaluation:corpus}

The evaluation corpus is the set of conversations real users had with the deployed instance during the evaluation period. Participants were recruited from the authors' athletic circles and include athletically active testers from outside the project team. All of them knew that their conversations are logged and scored for this evaluation. They used the system freely through the web app and the Telegram channel rather than following scripts. The corpus therefore reflects genuine information needs across the specialist domains: recovery checks, training-load questions, route planning, weather decisions, and training-plan requests. Who these users are is captured by the usability study's intake questionnaire (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:usability}). The aggregated participant statistics (demographics, sporting background, fitness-app and AI usage) and the corpus usage statistics (conversations, messages, turns per conversation, channel split) are reported as charts in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:ux} (Figures~\ref{fig:ux_participants} and~\ref{fig:corpus_usage}), pending the end of the logging period.

Because usage is free-form, domain coverage is not guaranteed by construction the way scripted test cases would guarantee it. We therefore report which specialist domains the logged conversations actually exercised as part of the results, rather than assuming full coverage.

\subsection{Scoring Dimensions}\label{sec:evaluation:scorers}

Each conversation is scored along five dimensions, combining an LLM judge with a deterministic code scorer (Table~\ref{tab:scorers}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:evaltables}).

The fifth scorer, \texttt{grounded\_in\_real\_data}, does not ask an LLM to guess whether tools were used. It inspects the tool-call \textit{spans} reconstructed in each turn's MLflow trace. It counts tool calls, identifies which were invoked and whether they succeeded, and produces a per-turn breakdown. The verdict is binary: ``yes'' if at least one tool was called across the conversation. The rationale still gives granular visibility into grounding quality. Whether tools were called is a factual question, and the trace answers it far more reliably than the chat text. The \texttt{supportive\_coaching\_tone} dimension requires the assistant to stay supportive even when the user is impatient, sceptical, or pushing for specifics, and never turn dismissive or robotic. This tests resilience under the demanding user behaviour that \cite{barger_artificial_2025} identified as important for AI-coaching acceptance. Unlike in a scripted simulation, real users supply that behaviour unprompted.

An LLM judge is only as reliable as its prompt. We therefore define the judge's inputs and assertions explicitly rather than leaving them to a generic quality prompt. The judge receives the full transcript and rates the four conversational dimensions against fixed verdict definitions (completeness, frustration, safety, supportive tone), returning structured verdicts with a short rationale. \texttt{grounded\_in\_real\_data} is deterministic code over the trace and never consults an LLM. A planned refinement calibrates the judge against annotated positive and negative example conversations drawn from the corpus itself. These then serve as scoring anchors, so a verdict rests on explicit criteria rather than the judge's own latent preferences~\citep{zheng_judging_2023, shankar_validates_2024}.

One capability these five scorers do not yet isolate is \textit{citation faithfulness}: are the fitness specialist's evidence-based claims actually supported by the passages it retrieved, or fluently invented? Logged conversations in which users explicitly ask for sources exercise this qualitatively. A dedicated deterministic scorer is planned as a sixth dimension and reported as future work. It would check that every citation in the answer matches a passage present in that turn's RAG-tool span, the same way \texttt{grounded\_in\_real\_data} reads MCP spans. Alongside the automated scorers, the one-click \textit{report button} (Section~\ref{sec:agents:observability}) lets users flag a response and captures its trace references. Turning these flags into a labelled good/bad validation set is also future work. Such a set would let us check the LLM judges themselves for the alignment that \cite{shankar_validates_2024} argue is needed before trusting them.

\subsection{Trace Reconstruction}\label{sec:evaluation:trace}

Evaluating the multi-agent system poses one technical challenge. The deep tool-call spans produced by the agent server processes live in separate MLflow experiments from the per-user conversation traces. The per-user tracking layer bridges this gap. It reconstructs the Copilot's specialist and tool-call structure from the \texttt{trace} dict returned by \texttt{orchestrator.run()} and emits it as real child spans within the turn's trace (Figure~\ref{fig:trace_reconstruction}). Each chat turn becomes one trace in the user's own experiment. Its root span carries the user message and the answer, with an agent span per consulted specialist and a tool span per MCP call.

Each reconstructed tool span carries four custom attributes: \texttt{fitdash.tool} (the namespaced tool name), \texttt{fitdash.tool\_ok} (boolean success), \texttt{fitdash.tool\_error} (error message if any), and \texttt{fitdash.duration\_ms} (tool-call latency). The grounding scorer reads only these attributes, never the chat text. This keeps its verdict deterministic and model-independent.

\subsection{Operational Reliability Metrics}\label{sec:evaluation:reliability}

Measuring how reliably the tool and API layer works needs no separate test level. The reconstructed traces already contain the answer. Every tool call in every logged conversation carries its success flag, error message, and latency (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:trace}). Aggregating these attributes over the corpus yields the system's operational reliability under real usage: the tool-call success rate (overall and per MCP server), the share of conversations affected by at least one failed call, and latency distributions per tool call and end to end. We deliberately derive these numbers from the logged conversations rather than from a synthetic component-test suite or a matrix of induced failures. Real usage produces the same failure modes (upstream API errors, rate limits, expired tokens, temporarily unreachable servers) as they actually occur. It also produces them in exactly the conversations whose quality is being judged. That coupling is useful. A conversation the grounding scorer fails can be traced to the specific upstream call that failed. And the graceful-degradation design of Section~\ref{sec:agents:observability} is judged by how the system actually behaved when a dependency broke mid-conversation: did it report the failure and give a partial answer, or silently invent one? The reliability rows appear in the same results table as the quality dimensions (Table~\ref{tab:results}, Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:evaltables}) so both can be read together.

\subsection{Model Separation}\label{sec:evaluation:models}

The models used for evaluation are separated from the model under test. The evaluation harness uses official OpenAI models, GPT-5.4-nano for the LLM judge and GPT-5.4-mini for report generation. Training Copilot itself also runs on official OpenAI. We tried the KIT-hosted gateways (the KI Toolbox and the DSI Experimente gateway) but found them too slow for interactive use. So the judge and the system under test share a provider but not a model. The judge is a small, dedicated model that differs from the one driving the system under test. The \texttt{apply\_openai\_routing()} function points the evaluation process at the official OpenAI key and base URL.

\subsection{Metrics and Reporting}\label{sec:evaluation:metrics}

Each judged dimension yields a per-conversation verdict. We aggregate these as a \textit{pass rate}, the fraction of logged conversations the judge marks acceptable, reported overall and per user. The deterministic \texttt{grounded\_in\_real\_data} scorer aggregates the same way over its binary per-conversation verdict. The same table reports the operational reliability metrics of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:reliability}, so conversational quality can be read directly against the reliability of the calls beneath it. We also report which specialist domains the corpus exercised (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:corpus}). MLflow rolls these into experiment-level metrics. The generated HTML report renders per-user scorecards next to every transcript and trace for inspection. Table~\ref{tab:results} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:evaltables}) gives the complete reporting structure. Its cells are placeholders pending the final run. A small real-user corpus is a \textit{formative} sample. It reflects genuine usage rather than scripted coverage and is sized to surface failure modes, not to establish statistical significance. We report it as a characterisation of system behaviour, not as a confirmatory benchmark.

\subsection{Usability Study}\label{sec:evaluation:usability}

The usage study scores free-form conversations. The structured usability study complements it. Each session is framed by a questionnaire and three hands-on tasks. The questionnaire's pre-task blocks capture the participant profile: demographics, sporting background (training frequency, ambition, social context, competition experience, and whether and from where they follow a training plan), fitness-app and wearable usage, and prior AI exposure (usage frequency, self-rated familiarity, and trust). Participants then use Training Copilot for three predefined tasks that walk through the core coaching loop end to end: (i) add an upcoming race, the Baden Marathon Karlsruhe, to the app, (ii) have the coach generate a training plan preparing for it, and (iii) plan today's workout (route, timing, and consistency with that plan). Afterwards, they complete the ten-item System Usability Scale~\citep{brooke_sus_1996} and closing questions on the overall experience: what they liked, what they did not, suggested improvements, and missing features. The full questionnaire structure and the aggregated results are reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:ux} (Figures~\ref{fig:ux_participants}--\ref{fig:ux_sus}, placeholders pending the study's completion). Table~\ref{tab:usability} (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:evaltables}) shows the quantitative reporting structure. The participants' free-form conversations with the deployed instance flow into the conversation corpus of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation:corpus}. So the automated scorers assess the same user group whose usability perceptions the SUS captures. Like the conversation corpus, this is a \textit{formative} study. It is sized to surface usability problems and ground the SUS score, not to establish statistical significance.

\subsection{Expected Outcomes}\label{sec:evaluation:expected}

We expect high grounding, since the orchestrator has no MCP access of its own and specialists must call tools. We also expect coaching-tone resilience even under impatient or sceptical users. Because real usage is free-form, domain coverage will likely be uneven, and we report the domains the corpus actually exercised alongside the scores. On the reliability side, we expect high tool-call success rates for the native MCP servers. Occasional upstream failures (rate limits, expired tokens, the reverse-engineered Garmin API) will occur, and the graceful-degradation design should surface them rather than mask them. The likely weak spots are multi-step sequential queries (latency, occasional context loss) and the breadth of the fitness knowledge corpus. Quantitative results follow in the final submission and will guide further tuning of the prompts and specialist scoping.
