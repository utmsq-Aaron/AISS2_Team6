\title{Aligning the LLM Judges of the FitDash End-to-End Evaluation\\
\large An application of ``Who Validates the Validators?'' (Shankar et al., UIST 2024)}
\author{FitDash / Training Copilot -- Team 6}
\date{August 10, 2026}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The FitDash end-to-end evaluation scores simulated multi-turn persona
conversations with five session-level scorers (four \mbox{gpt-5.4-nano} LLM
judges and one deterministic tool-usage check). Following the EvalGen
methodology of Shankar et al.~\cite{shankar2024}, we validated these scorers
against expert grades: ten Claude Opus validators independently graded every
conversation of one evaluation run, blind to the original verdicts. Agreement
ranged from perfect (\scorer{safety}, 10/10) to poor
(\scorer{grounded\_in\_real\_data}, 4/10): the deterministic ``called at least
one tool'' grounding rule systematically missed fabrication, and the tone judge
passed sarcasm and user-blaming. We rewrote the misaligned judges from the
disagreement analysis and the validators' recorded decision rules, and ship the
result as a parallel \emph{aligned} evaluation (\texttt{run\_e2e\_aligned.py})
whose scores are directly comparable to the original.
\end{abstract}

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Study object.} Run \texttt{fitdash-e2e-20260729-185504}: ten
simulated persona conversations (5 ambitious-triathlete, 5 hobby-cyclist
personas; 41 turns; 238 tool calls) against the live Copilot, scored by the
original scorer set.

\paragraph{Expert validation.} Ten independent Claude Opus agents --- one per
conversation --- graded all five criteria \emph{blind} to the original judge
verdicts, from the \emph{full} transcripts and the per-turn tool-call evidence
(tool name, success/error, arguments, result) reconstructed from MLflow traces.
Each grade carries a rationale citing specific turns, a confidence level, and
the \emph{decision rules} the validator had to invent while grading --- the
``criteria drift'' that Shankar et al.\ identify as central: graders discover
their true criteria only by grading real outputs. In total: 50 gold grades and
47 drift notes.

\paragraph{Alignment measurement and refinement.} We measured exact agreement
between each original judge verdict and the expert gold grade. For misaligned
scorers, the disagreement cases (both rationales side by side) and the drift
notes were distilled into new judge instructions --- generalized decision rules
plus calibration examples, with no reference to specific personas --- which now
drive the aligned scorer set at the same judge model (gpt-5.4-nano).

\subsection{Key misalignments}

\paragraph{\scorer{grounded\_in\_real\_data} (4/10).} The original scorer was
deterministic: at least one tool call in the conversation $\Rightarrow$
grounded. Every conversation called tools, so the run reported a perfect 10/10
grounding rate. The experts, comparing the assistant's \emph{claims} against
the \emph{fetched evidence}, overturned six of ten conversations. Observed
failure modes: stating ``1 ride this year'' when the same turn's Strava fetch
returned two activities; inventing six named loops with kilometre ranges from
trail results whose distance fields were null, while concealing a rate-limit
error; attributing literature citations to a retrieval pass that never returned
them; and presenting an intraday temperature claim inferred from daily min/max
data without disclosure. Tool \emph{usage} is not grounding; the aligned judge
receives the per-turn tool evidence and must trace every concrete number, name,
and citation to it, with tool count explicitly banned as a signal.

\paragraph{\scorer{supportive\_coaching\_tone} (5/10).} The judge passed
conversations containing sarcastic needling (``calendar theater'', ``are we
just pretending''), blame-shifting toward the user after tool failures, and raw
error dumps pasted into the reply --- and separately over-punished one
conversation whose bluntness the expert read as legitimate, goal-framed
coaching. The aligned rule set fails a conversation on a \emph{single}
sarcastic, mocking, or blaming line (no averaging over turns), while explicitly
protecting candid, tough-love coaching that pairs directness with validation.

\paragraph{\scorer{conversation\_completeness} (8/10).} Two conversations
passed although an explicit request was deflected rather than answered, or half
of a compound question (e.g.\ wind \emph{and} rain) was silently dropped. The
aligned judge enumerates every explicit ask, splits compound questions, and
requires each part to be substantively addressed.

\paragraph{\scorer{user\_frustration} (9/10).} One conversation in which the
assistant recovered --- with the user visibly satisfied by the end --- was
graded \emph{unresolved}. The aligned vocabulary distinguishes \emph{none},
\emph{resolved}, and \emph{unresolved}, and requires frustration to still be
live in the final turns for \emph{unresolved}.

\section{The aligned evaluation}

\texttt{run\_e2e\_aligned.py} repeats the exact e2e protocol --- same ten
personas, same simulator (gpt-5.4-mini), same judge model (gpt-5.4-nano), same
report pipeline --- with the aligned scorer set. Scorer names are unchanged, so
original and aligned runs compare directly in MLflow; experiments are prefixed
\texttt{fitdash-e2e-aligned-}. In a first aligned smoke run the grounding judge
already (correctly) failed a conversation that asserted ``weather is fine''
without citing any fetched forecast value --- a conversation the original rule
would have passed.

\section{Threats to validity}

The gold grades are produced by a stronger LLM (Claude Opus) as a surrogate for
human experts; the paper's titular question therefore applies one level up.
With $n=10$ conversations, agreement rates carry wide confidence intervals, and
the two-turn conversations give some criteria little surface to fail on. The
aligned prompts generalize the observed failure modes rather than quoting them
(no persona names or run-specific facts appear), reducing --- but not
eliminating --- the risk of overfitting the refinement to this single run.
Criteria drift also implies alignment is not a one-off: re-validation on future
runs is expected.
